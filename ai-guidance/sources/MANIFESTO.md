# The Agentic Polymodality Manifesto

*Giving agents identity. Preserving meaning across contexts.*

## A world worth representing

A person can be a customer, a colleague, an account owner, and a member of a community. A document can be a source of evidence, a contractual obligation, and a sensitive resource. A project can carry a purpose, a history, a budget, and responsibilities to the people it affects.

These perspectives coexist. Each reveals something important about the same world.

As agents begin to represent people, organizations, projects, and services, they need a way to carry those distinctions into their work. They need identities that endure, meanings that remain intelligible, and relationships that can be examined.

We call the pursuit of this infrastructure **agentic polymodality**.

Agentic polymodality is an architectural approach for giving agents explicit identities and context-specific representations, with defined relationships and translations between them. It makes the dimensions of agent participation independently expressible and connects them when agents collaborate.

## The moment meaning slips

Three agents are working on a customer request.

The sales agent says the customer is qualified. The finance agent says the customer is approved. The security agent says the customer is authorized.

The fulfillment agent receives the word “approved” and proceeds.

But approved for what? Credit? A discount? Access to a restricted service? Who made the decision, under which rules, and for how long?

Each agent may have understood its own task. The handoff still lost the distinctions that made its conclusion meaningful.

Now imagine that every contribution carries its source, subject, interpretation, scope, and applicable authority. The fulfillment agent can identify the approval it needs, discover which decision is missing, and request it from the appropriate representative.

The promise is practical: agents can coordinate while preserving the meaning of their contributions.

## Representation deserves structure

Prompts give agents instructions. Durable representation also needs explicit structure: what an agent represents, the context it inhabits, the claims it can make, and the relationships through which it participates.

A growing system should let those properties evolve deliberately. A vocabulary can change while an identity persists. Access can be revoked while a contribution retains its provenance. A new responsibility can be assigned with a clear scope.

Dimensional representation gives us a language for those changes.

Here, a dimension is an independently expressible aspect of representation or action. Identity, semantics, context, governance, and cybersecurity are useful starting dimensions. Their relationships must be defined explicitly; independence is a design goal, and mathematical orthogonality requires a formal model.

## Our commitments

### 1. Give representation an identity

An agent should be able to represent an identifiable person, organization, project, service, or other entity. Its own identity and the identity of what it represents should be distinguishable. Delegation should have a source, scope, and lifecycle.

This makes it possible to ask whose perspective an agent expresses and whose authority supports an action.

### 2. Keep meaning connected to context

A claim should carry the context needed to interpret it: its subject, vocabulary, source, assumptions, and relevant conditions.

When an agent says “approved,” collaborators should be able to determine what was approved and what consequences follow. Shared terminology becomes more useful when its interpretation is explicit.

### 3. Make dimensions independently expressible

An agent’s semantics, governance, and cybersecurity each deserve their own representation and mechanisms.

Semantics describes what a contribution means. Governance describes responsibilities, decision rights, and accountability. Cybersecurity controls access to resources and operations.

Their connections matter. A meaningful statement of authority must connect to verifiable delegation and enforcement. Keeping these aspects distinct lets us examine and change each one deliberately.

### 4. Make translation a visible relationship

Agents working in different contexts need explicit mappings between concepts, claims, and decisions. A translation should preserve provenance and disclose assumptions, uncertainty, and information loss where relevant.

Some perspectives can be reconciled. Others require another decision or a person’s judgment. The architecture should make those boundaries visible.

### 5. Preserve continuity through change

Models, tools, instructions, and organizational roles will change. Represented entities need continuity across those changes.

Versioned contexts, traceable contributions, and explicit delegation can help agents maintain useful relationships over time. A collaborator should be able to establish which identity and interpretation applied when a contribution was made.

### 6. Make the benefits observable

The project must earn confidence through examples that others can inspect, reproduce, and challenge.

We should demonstrate whether explicit representation reduces ambiguous handoffs, preserves scope, improves traceability, and supports changes across platforms. We should measure the additional complexity and document where simpler approaches work well.

## The infrastructure we want to build

We envision interoperable building blocks for identities, contextual representations, scoped claims, delegation, and translation. These should be understandable enough for developers to implement and precise enough for independent systems to exchange.

Existing schemas, protocols, identity systems, and policy mechanisms provide foundations. The work is to define how they fit together so that meaning and responsibility survive an agent handoff. Representing a permission also requires a connection to a mechanism that enforces it.

Our first demonstration should be small: several agents representing different perspectives on one entity, exchanging a scoped decision, and resolving a missing or ambiguous approval. Its success should be evident in the resulting behavior and the inspectable record.

## An invitation

Bring a situation where agents need to understand the same thing from different perspectives. Bring a failure that lost meaning at a boundary. Bring a schema, an integration, a counterexample, or a better definition.

Together, we can give agents the infrastructure to represent a world with many perspectives—and to act within it with intelligible meaning, explicit responsibility, and enforceable boundaries.

**Let identity endure. Let context travel. Let collaboration preserve meaning.**
